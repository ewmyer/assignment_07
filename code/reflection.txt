I learned that scraping depends on understanding how a page is built, not just
finding one CSS selector. Class and id selectors helped locate elements, while a
sibling selector was useful for connecting a section heading to its menu cards.
The sites also required different approaches: reading visible text with
`inner_text`, reading an item's `href` attribute, waiting for JavaScript-loaded
content, and handling pagination while crawling. For tabular menus, `read_html`
and `melt` made it easier to reshape size columns into one row per item and size.

The matching work taught me that fuzzy matching measures similarity between
strings, not whether two products are actually the same. Normalization removes
formatting differences, and a score threshold can identify likely matches, but
names like "Matcha Latte" and "Mocha Latte" show why a human in the loop matters.
Saving owner decisions also prevents asking the same questions on the next run.

In the analysis, I learned to use an outer merge with a merge indicator to
separate items sold only by us, only by rivals, or by both. I found it important
to group rival menus by distinct café before counting availability, so multiple
sizes do not inflate the gap counts. One area I want to practice more is choosing
the right pandas operation and checking its row order after merges and
aggregations. Next, I will recreate a small example dataset, predict the merge
and groupby results by hand, and compare them with the actual output and tests.
